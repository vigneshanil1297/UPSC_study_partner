# Topic: The basic structure doctrine is one of the most potent tools of the judiciary for maintaining the checks and balances for smooth functioning of democracy. Comment.

The **doctrine of basic structure** is a *judicial review* doctrine used to check the constitutional validity of amendment acts. The philosophy behind it is to establish the difference between **amending** and **re-writing** the Constitution — because amendments are made by Parliament, which represents *political sovereignty*, while the Constituent Assembly that wrote the Constitution represented *popular sovereignty and the general will*.

The doctrine arose from the debate over the **amendability powers** of Parliament under *Article 368*. The Supreme Court, through a series of judgments — *Shankari Prasad*, *I.C. Golaknath case 1967* — tried to define these powers. Finally, in the **Kesavananda Bharati case (1973)**, the Court held that Parliament under A.368 can amend *any and every part* of the Constitution, *subject to the basic structure doctrine*. The doctrine is thus an **innovation of the judiciary** that goes beyond the written part — the *metaphysical part* of the Constitution.

Indian democracy rests on three pillars — *Executive, Legislature, Judiciary* — working in conjunction; India follows **checks and balances**, not a strict separation of powers. The Court has used the doctrine to strike down overreaching steps of the executive — e.g. the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015 and the NJAC Act** were struck down for violating the *independence of the judiciary*. The Court has not fixed a closed list of basic-structure elements, opting for *creative interpretation* as and when required.

This has been criticised as **judicial activism / overreach**; but one must understand that the doctrine ensures the smooth functioning of democracy by **avoiding the tyranny of the majority** — making it a genuine instrument of checks and balances rather than a usurpation.
