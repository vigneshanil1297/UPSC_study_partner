# Topic: The past will not stand up for itself, but we can

Why distort a past that can no longer stand up for itself? "Two roads diverged in a yellow wood, and sorry I could not travel both" — these lines by Robert Frost perfectly signify the struggle of a present that will one day become a past. The difference between the two is that in the present we have a choice to make, but once a decision is taken it becomes our past, which can only be changed by a future course of action and never by revisiting the past.

The characteristic feature of the past is that it is a series of events already lived in time and space, and it places in front of us certain facts which will always remain facts and not disappear on account of our likes and dislikes. So, what do we understand — is history static? Partly yes, because we cannot change certain variables; but partly no, because there is always a scope for interpretation. "A glass half-full" or "a glass half-empty" is all about the reference frame, the perception and the varied interpretation. Though the past is open for interpretation, the truth won't change — but then we must understand there is no single truth; there is a *regime of truth*.

For example, India has been a colonial slave for almost two centuries, but the British claim it to be their civilisational mission and the "white man's burden." Here we have a single event interpreted by two different schools of thought, totally differently. But who could answer this best — the one who suffered, or the one who caused the suffering? The past definitely cannot stand up for itself and answer it, but it has left behind a trail of incidents that help us verify the facts and arrive at the most probable truth. Indians who suffered the wrath of colonial rule were "rewarded" with the British civilising mission in the form of huge poverty, underdevelopment, famines and a dead economy. The sufferings of Mother India were to an extent that after Independence the future looked all dark and clueless — but salute to the efforts of our Constitution-makers and visionaries who traced a path that has led India to become a leader of the Global South, standing tall at its own pole. The only problem with the past is that it won't stand up for itself; but should this provide us the right to distort it or to change it? It all depends on the way we look at it.

Now, let us look at the environment today. Recently the UNGA chief António Guterres remarked that "the era of global warming has ended; the era of global boiling has arrived." The issue of climate change is not new — it traces its roots to the Industrial Revolution of the 17th and 18th centuries — yet the attention of the international community was drawn only in the last decade of the 20th century. Since then negotiations have been carried out, but little consensus has been achieved between the developed and developing countries, where both accuse each other for their negligence in the past. But what is the point digging at the past when this urgent crisis requires a future course of action to stop the mishap? Definitely the past won't stand up and clarify who has abused the environment more; but all we know is, the present and future demand action and not negligence.

In the same way, the historical injustice suffered by transgenders has been justified as a "time-tested practice" dating back to ancient times that considers homosexuality unnatural. So what is to be done — just let it happen because it has been happening since the past, or try changing the course of thinking in the present and mould our future? The guarantor of constitutional morality, our Supreme Court, decided to go on with the second option and struck down Section 377 of the IPC, thereby decriminalising homosexuality and ensuring a life of dignity for all irrespective of gender and sexual orientation.

The past can be viewed either as an iron inscription or as a mirror to our present and a gateway to the past. We must choose it wisely — not just to suit our interest or justify our acts, but to ensure that earth becomes a better place to live. Today, in the 21st century, the world is witnessing events like war (Russia–Ukraine), an arms race (nuclear weapons), famine (Sudan) and state-sponsored terrorism, and all these evils have their roots in the past. Many things have unfolded and are known to us — the reasons for strife between the US and USSR, why Afghanistan became a breeding ground for terrorism — but there are still certain mysteries of the past that are yet unfolded and unknown, which gives us an opportunity to look at the present to understand the past. That past anyway won't stand to provide justification for any incident, but it will at least give us a lesson for the future and help us differentiate between short-term actions and long-term gains.

There is no need to mould and distort the past to justify our actions; rather it is time we humans start reflecting on our activities and accept that we need to change for the good, instead of looking at things with the purpose of a blame-game. History won't stand for itself — but we can.
